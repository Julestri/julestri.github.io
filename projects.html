What I’m working on

OsHV-1 genomes sampled across French oyster production areas over time retain a record of where and when the virus has spread. By combining viral genome sequences with sampling dates and geographic coordinates, it is possible to reconstruct the virus's dispersal routes and estimate how frequently it moved between oyster farming areas. The second part of this work aims to link the reconstructed viral dispersal patterns with environmental and anthropogenic factors to identify the main drivers shaping OsHV-1 spread at large spatial scales. This approach allows to assess the contribution of well-established drivers, such as seawater temperature, while also investigating the potential role of factors that remain largely hypothetical, such as the influence of live oyster movements occurring at the national scale. By integrating both environmental conditions and human-mediated connectivity, this framework seeks to disentangle the relative importance of natural and anthropogenic processes in shaping the spatial diffusion of the virus across oyster production areas.

Following the calibration and validation of a compartmental epidemiological model at the scale of a local oyster population, the next step is to extend this framework to the scale of an entire oyster farming area. The objective is to develop a spatially explicit epidemiological model capable of simulating OsHV-1 outbreaks across interconnected farming sites and evaluating the effectiveness of potential disease management strategies through scenario-based simulations. To achieve this, each oyster farming parcel (defined from the official cadastral map) is represented as an individual epidemiological unit governed by its own compartmental model. These local units are then coupled through the transport of viral particles between sites using a hydrodynamic-informed viral dispersal model, allowing the simulation of pathogen transmission driven by seawater circulation. The spatial model will be calibrated and validated using occurrence data collected during a large-scale field survey conducted in 2025. Once validated, it will provide a simulation framework to investigate epidemic dynamics, identify key transmission pathways, and assess the potential impact of alternative management strategies on disease spread across oyster production areas.

Previous projects

The model was developed from the classical SEIR (Susceptible–Exposed–Infectious–Recovered) compartmental framework commonly used in epidemiological modelling. To adapt this approach to OsHV-1 infection in Pacific oysters, we introduced two key extensions. First, an additional W (Water) compartment was included to explicitly represent the viral concentration in the surrounding environment and account for environmental virus-mediated transmission. Second, the susceptible compartment was split into two infection pathways, distinguishing between oysters that survive the infection and those that die following infection. This structure makes it possible to explicitly represent the heterogeneous outcomes of OsHV-1 infection within an oyster population. All model parameters were estimated from controlled experimental infection trials specifically designed to characterise OsHV-1 infection dynamics. Parameter distributions were then refined using an Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) approach. This process resulted in a calibrated and validated epidemiological model that was subsequently used for global sensitivity analysis. The sensitivity analysis highlighted the key role of surviving individuals in shaping OsHV-1 epidemic dynamics, emphasising the importance of explicitly accounting for survival following infection when modelling epidemics caused by this virus.

Fisheries research programmes rely on sampling campaigns during which large numbers of fish have to be identified to species level, since the ecological status of each stock is assessed from those counts. Identification is done by hand and is the slowest step of the process. This master's project, carried out with researchers from Institut Agro Rennes, asked whether recent progress in image recognition could be used to automate it, and produced a tool for rapid image processing and species identification. Rather than building a network from scratch, we started from YOLO, an architecture already designed and parameterised for object detection, and fine-tuned it on a training set of annotated fish images. The resulting tool identified species with roughly 2% error, a level that makes it usable as a first-pass identification aid for research institutes and fisheries professionals.
